How to think about growth and the marketing role at Neoflo

Before you hire a CMO, decide what the role has to solve

This is a coaching note, not a business plan. It sets out how we'd frame the decisions in front of you, the options you have, and the risks to watch. It's based only on our conversation, so any numbers are reference points to test, not targets.

Summary

Neoflo's product is proven: well-known brands pay for it, and roundtables reliably turn the right conversations into customers. The bottleneck is that growth still runs through you. Your network creates the meetings, your follow-up moves the deals, and your judgement fills the gaps a system would normally cover. Hiring a senior marketing leader won't remove that bottleneck on its own. What removes it is being clear about the work the role has to do, staging the investment so you keep flexibility, and watching the right signals so you can adjust early.

We'd suggest framing your decisions around four questions:

  1. What work does the marketing role actually need to cover? Define it by the jobs, not the title, then decide who does each one.
  2. How much should you invest, and how fast? Treat the budget as a ceiling released in stages, and spread it across proven channels, scaling bets and experiments.
  3. Which risks matter now, and which build up over time? Short-term risks cost deals this quarter. Long-term risks quietly cap how big Neoflo can get.
  4. How will you know it's working, and when should you change course? With a seven-to-nine-month cycle, revenue reports too late. You need leading signals.

1Define the marketing role by the work, not the title

The instinct when growth stalls is to hire a CMO. But the title describes a level of seniority, not the work Neoflo needs done. From our conversation, that work falls into four areas: positioning and messaging, because the pitch may not match how finance buyers want to hear it; demand engines that create first meetings without relying on your network alone; pipeline nurture, which keeps long deals warm and brings hidden objections to the surface; and ecosystem and partnerships, including integrations and AI-platform programmes.

Taken together, this is roughly 20% strategy and 80% oversight and execution. That's why a traditional full-time CMO is often a poor fit at this stage. They are paid mainly for strategy, tend to work at a different pace from an early-stage company, and are not always fluent in the AI-assisted way this work is now done. You have real flexibility in how you cover it:

Option A: a hands-on senior director. This is the simplest if you can find one who is comfortable doing as well as directing. It's the hardest profile to find, and an expensive one to get wrong.

Option B: divide the role. You keep ecosystem and partnerships, which only a founder can open. An advisor sets up and oversees the demand and nurture engines. A mid-level, AI-forward team executes. This gets you the most flexibility for the least fixed cost, but it needs your time to coordinate.

Option C: hire the operator first. Start with the mid-level team and add senior guidance as gaps appear. This is the fastest to start, but it risks a team without direction if positioning is still unsettled.

Whichever you choose, someone inside Neoflo has to own messaging. That's the one job you can't fully outsource.

2Stage the investment so you keep your options open

A useful reference point for a venture-backed company aiming to double is to put 20–25% of revenue into sales and marketing, people included. The important part is how you release it. Size the budget from your growth plan, then release it quarter by quarter against actual revenue. That way it works as a ceiling you can grow into, not a commitment you have to defend. If revenue runs ahead, you can accelerate. If it lags, you slow down without having over-invested.

Within the budget, think of channels as a portfolio. Roughly 70% goes to what's proven: for Neoflo, that's roundtables (every two weeks is a sustainable rhythm) and the content they generate. About 20% goes to bets with early signal, such as AI visibility, because buyers increasingly research vendors through AI tools. About 10% goes to experiments that are allowed to fail, such as outbound. Experiments are judged on whether they show a signal, not on return in the first quarter. Outbound is an acquisition cost, not cost of goods sold. It's reasonable to fund it from marketing while it's a test and move it to sales once it works. The split matters less than the discipline: every channel has a stated purpose and a point at which you'll decide whether to keep it.

3Separate the short-term risks from the long-term ones

The risks in front of Neoflo don't all have the same timescale, and mixing them up leads to the wrong fixes. Short-term risks cost deals now and are mostly fixed with process. Long-term risks build up quietly and limit how far the company can grow; they need positioning and structure.

4Watch leading signals, and know when to change course

With a seven-to-nine-month cycle, revenue tells you whether last year's decisions worked, not this quarter's. Judge progress on leading signals instead: first meetings booked by channel, qualified opportunities for each customer tier, conversion from statement of work to pilot, the reasons deals are lost, how visible Neoflo is in AI answers for its key buyer questions, and how leads respond to nurture. Real-time and roughly right is more useful here than precise and late.

The signals also tell you what to adjust, which is often not the channel itself. If roundtables produce meetings but few pilots, the problem is more likely the message than the event. If outbound gets replies but no meetings, look at targeting before the volume. If AI visibility rises but inbound doesn't follow, the content may not answer the questions buyers actually ask. Set each test's target before it starts, review at three and six months, and decide deliberately whether to keep it, change it or stop it.

5Questions to work through together

  1. Which of the four areas of work do you want to own personally, and which would you rather hand off?
  2. What growth plan do you want the budget to follow, and how fast are you willing to release it?
  3. Where in the pipeline do deals most often stall today, and what does the past year's data say about why?
  4. Who inside Neoflo could own messaging, and which partners or communities could you open first?

Customers' requests for marketing analytics, and the broader integration strategy, each deserve their own conversation.
