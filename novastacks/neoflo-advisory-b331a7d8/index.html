Navigating Neoflo's next stage of growth

In a new, sticky category, the bigger risk is moving too slowly

A coaching note, not a business plan. It's based only on our conversation, so treat it as a way of framing your decisions rather than a set of answers.

Summary

Neoflo's product is proven, but growth still runs through you. Well-known brands pay for it, roundtables turn the right conversations into customers, and once integrated, clients stay. What limits growth now is not the product but how fast Neoflo can put itself in front of the right buyers, before competitors integrate first.

Three decisions matter most:

  1. Strategy: make next quarter about the funnel. Bring in more upper-funnel demand and move it into qualified, middle-funnel conversations. Judge the quarter on that movement, not on revenue.
  2. Budget: plan quarter by quarter, and invest above the usual 10–20% of revenue. The opportunity cost and the likely lifetime value justify it. Secure the predictable roundtables first, and cap riskier bets so they buy data, not losses.
  3. Resources: hire for visibility, and let the interview decide the level. If a CMO candidate can't coach you, hire a marketing director to execute your vision. Either way, choose speed over the perfect hire.

Everything else in this note is operational and follows from these three.

1 · Strategy

Next quarter's goal: fill the upper funnel and move it into the middle

The cost of moving slowly is higher than it looks. Neoflo sells into a category that is still forming. Contracts are large, the product integrates into clients' systems, and customers rarely leave. That makes each account worth winning early: once a competitor is integrated, that account is effectively closed for years. Referrals alone can't fill the pipeline at the pace this window requires.

For the next quarter, measure success by funnel movement, not revenue. With a seven-to-nine-month cycle, revenue reflects decisions made last year. The goal now is two-fold. First, widen the upper funnel: more of the right finance and e-commerce leaders aware of Neoflo and in a first conversation, through roundtables, content, AI visibility, your own network and a contained outbound test. Second, move that demand into the middle funnel: qualified opportunities that progress to a statement of work and pilot, supported by consistent follow-up, nurture and sharper messaging.

Track the signals that show the funnel is moving, and adjust to what they reveal. These are first meetings by channel, qualified opportunities by customer tier, conversion from statement of work to pilot, and the reasons deals are lost. The signals often point to something other than the channel. Roundtables that produce meetings but few pilots usually mean a messaging problem, not an event problem.

2 · Budget

Plan quarter by quarter, and invest above the usual share of revenue

Look at marketing spend one quarter at a time, not as a full-year budget. Mature B2B companies typically spend 10–20% of revenue on marketing. Because the opportunity cost is high for Neoflo, you should go above that. How far above can't be known yet. The way to find out is to invest enough to get real data points: how many leads each line of spend produces, and whether those leads convert.

Early signs suggest lifetime value is well above today's revenue per client. Customers integrate Neoflo into their systems, stay, and are likely to expand. Budgeting against current revenue per client therefore under-invests by design. Early spend should be judged on what it teaches you, not on breaking even in its first quarter.

Secure the predictable spend first, and cap the risky bets. CFO roundtables give you the most predictable pipeline at the lowest cost, so budget them with confidence and protect them as the priority. Outbound and AI visibility are riskier. Invest in them to learn, but keep each one below about $20K a quarter so the downside stays contained.

Keep it disciplined with three habits. Have a business analyst model customer acquisition cost and lifetime value under several growth scenarios. Set a guardrail tied to current revenue so spending never outruns what the business can carry. And review the same metrics every quarter, so each quarter's estimates, and the budget that follows from them, are better than the last.

Before committing money, make sure someone can run it. Two or three internal managers should be able to work with the vendors, keep everyone moving at the same pace, and interview for the senior hire. With it in place, a first quarter could look like this:

3 · Resources

Hire for visibility, and let the interview decide the level

The senior marketing role exists to give you visibility. Its job is to run the engines that show you, week by week, who your audience is, where the pipeline stands and where Neoflo is visible. Without someone doing that daily, the picture stays fragmented and every decision stays with you.

Use the interview to choose between a CMO and a marketing director. A CMO worth hiring should share your vision and, in the interview itself, be able to coach you: sharpen how you think about the buyer, the market and the order of moves. If no candidate does that, step back. What you need is someone to execute the vision you already hold: a B2B marketing director with a product marketing background and hands-on experience in marketing automation and nurture.

Optimise for speed, not the perfect hire. While you search, an advisor and a mid-level, AI-forward team can run the engines, so whoever joins inherits momentum. Messaging ownership must stay inside Neoflo.

Operational notes

  • Outbound accounting. It's an acquisition cost, not cost of goods sold. Fund it from marketing while it's a test, and move it to sales once it works.
  • Nurture. Send regular, relevant product updates to every lead and key partner. It should be owned in-house by a product marketer, not a vendor.
  • Pipeline hygiene. Keep one pipeline view, and give every deal a named next step and date. A short lost-deal survey brings out what ghosting hides.
  • Founder channels. Technology providers, industry bodies and communities, plus AI-platform partner programmes and whether an MCP integration makes sense.
